## 2. Architecture, in one breath (0:15–0:55)

**[ON SCREEN: a simple left-to-right diagram — user message → intent router →
four retrieval routes (keyword / category / vector / dense) → RRF fusion →
reranking (heuristic → optional frozen cross-encoder → optional LLM) →
recommendation. Label it with the four pillar names if there's room.]**

> Four pieces, matching the brief's four pillars.
>
> One: intent routing. Every turn is scored on how decisive the shopper's language
> is and how specified their request already is — buying-track sessions hard-gate
> to a structured filter once enough is known; a hard requirement like "under
> fifty dollars" gets enforced immediately, even on the browsing track.
>
> Two: multi-turn state. A slot-accumulation state machine tracks what's been said
> — and separately detects when the shopper *reverses* themselves, clearing only
> the slots that actually conflict.
>
> Three: dynamic context. The shopper's profile and session history get distilled
> into a compact signal every turn, driving a runtime decision — which retrieval
> routes, which reranker, when to just ask a clarifying question instead of
> guessing.
>
> Four: retrieval and reranking. Keyword, category, TF-IDF, and dense embeddings
> get fused by weighted reciprocal rank fusion, then reranked — by a fast local
> heuristic, an optional frozen cross-encoder, or an optional paid LLM. Optional
> is the operative word — the shipped default spends zero dollars.

## 4. How we know it actually works — the validation discipline (2:15–3:05)

**[ON SCREEN: a simple ladder chart — organizer baseline 0.107 → each validated
step upward → final 0.599. If a chart is too much production, just show the
"Results on the public dev set" table from README.md.]**

> Here's the part that matters more than any single number: nothing in this
> pipeline shipped on a hunch. Every change was validated by an A/B run — or,
> for anything with enough free parameters to overfit, five-fold cross-validation
> — against the official evaluator, before it was trusted.
>
> The single biggest win: changing when the agent stops asking clarifying
> questions, from a blunt two-strikes rule to one that only stops once nothing
> useful is left to ask. That alone moved the technical score from point-four-two
> to point-four-nine.
>
> Adding a dense semantic-embedding route fixed something specific: shoppers who
> reverse their intent mid-conversation. That track's hit rate jumped from
> thirty-seven to fifty percent — the cleanest, most isolated win in the whole
> project.
>
> And we're just as disciplined about the losses. We benchmarked a paid LLM
> reranker, hoping semantic judgment would help. It made things worse — a real,
> repeatable regression. We didn't just revert it — we root-caused it: reordering
> the exact same candidates flipped the model's top pick ninety-two-and-a-half
> percent of the time. That's a structural weakness in listwise scoring, not bad
> luck — so the frozen local cross-encoder we ship instead scores candidates one
> at a time, specifically to avoid that failure mode.
>
> Of twenty-one tuning attempts across this project, close to half came back
> negative or neutral — and every one of those got fully reverted, not shipped
> disabled behind a flag. That ratio is the honest cost of actually measuring
> instead of assuming.

## 5. Final numbers (3:05–3:35)

**[ON SCREEN: the headline table — organizer baseline vs. shipped default, all
four metrics.]**

> The shipped result on the two-hundred-session public set: a technical score of
> point-six-oh, a seventy-point-five percent hit rate, and a mean-reciprocal-rank
> of point-four-four — roughly five-and-a-half times the organizer's baseline
> agent on hit rate, six-and-a-half times on MRR.
>
> And the entire shipped default runs for zero dollars. No paid API is required
> — the frozen cross-encoder and dense retrieval are local, open-weight models,
> packaged and loaded fully offline. The optional LLM path, when someone does
> turn it on, reports its real token usage every turn — not a placeholder.

**[ON SCREEN: two bullets from the Limitations section.]**

> We're not going to pretend this is finished. Twenty-six of the two hundred

## Delivery notes

- **Pacing**: read Section 4 slightly slower than the rest — it's the section
  doing the most persuasive work ("Technical Execution" and "Innovation & Problem
  Insight" combined are 55% of the online-round score, per
  `docs/final_evaluation_faq.md` §6), and it's easy to rush past the one line that
  actually proves the claim (the 92.5% reordering-flip number).
- **If you only have 2 minutes**: keep Sections 1, 3 (pick *one* transcript, not
  both), and 5. Cut 2, 4, 6, 7 to one sentence each.
- **Don't round further than the script already does** — "point-six-oh" for 0.599
  is already a judgment call; rounding harder than that starts to feel like
  spin, which cuts against the "honest" goal more than a slightly less punchy
  number costs you.
